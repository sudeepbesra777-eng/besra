# 5-Minute Faceless Cut — "Under the Ice"

**Format:** 30 blocks × 10s = **5:00 exactly** (the faceless-video pipeline fixes duration at N×10s)
**Rule per block:** ONE narration line, **7.8–9.5s of detected speech** (~20–24 words at 150 wpm), centred in its block by the assembler.
**Aspect:** 16:9 · **Narration:** one locked voice, no on-camera presenter · **Subtitles:** burned

> Lines are written to length. If a take measures over 9.5s of speech, shorten that line — never
> stretch the block or time-stretch the audio.

| 01 | There's a hole in Antarctica. Two feet wide. It drops through six hundred metres of ice into water no light has ever touched. | Slow push-in on a black borehole in a white plain. Wind. No music yet. |
| 02 | In 2020, scientists lowered a robot down that hole. They wanted to measure melting. They found out they'd been measuring the wrong thing. | Yellow torpedo-shaped robot descending into the shaft. |
| 03 | The glacier is called Thwaites. Researchers have another name for it. They call it the Doomsday Glacier. That is not marketing. | Title card over satellite Antarctica, pin drop on the west coast. |
| 04 | Antarctica is not land with snow on top. It is rock, buried under an ice sheet up to four kilometres thick. | Cross-section diagram: bedrock, ice, ocean. |
| 05 | Stand on that surface, and the real ground sits further below you than a passenger jet flies above you. | Vertical scale comparison, figure on ice, jet above. |
| 06 | Thwaites itself is roughly the size of Great Britain. One glacier. Larger than most countries on Earth. And it is moving. | Map outline of Thwaites with Great Britain silhouette overlaid. |
| 07 | Here is what makes it dangerous. The rock beneath Thwaites sits below sea level, and it slopes downward as you travel inland. | Cross-section, bed sloping down and inland, sea-level line marked. |
| 08 | That means the ocean can reach underneath it. Warm water slides in from the sea and eats the glacier from below. | Warm-current arrows pushing under the ice base. |
| 09 | From above, nothing looks wrong. The surface is white, solid, still. For decades, that surface was all we could actually see. | Aerial drift over a featureless white plain. |
| 10 | So a British and American team flew onto the ice with a hot water drill, and started melting a hole straight down. | Field camp, drill rig, hose feeding into ice. |
| 11 | It took days. The borehole had to be kept warm the whole time, or it would freeze shut and swallow the equipment. | Hose descending, timer overlay, steam in cold air. |
| 12 | The robot is called Icefin. Three and a half metres long, thin enough to fit down a shaft barely wider than a dinner plate. | Icefin in profile, scale annotation against the borehole width. |
| 13 | It carries cameras, sonar, and sensors for temperature and salt. A cable feeds power down, and sends data back up. | Component callouts on the robot body, tether spooling. |
| 14 | Icefin reached the bottom, swam sideways into the cavity, and became the first machine to see where Thwaites meets the sea. | Robot emerging from the shaft into open dark water, lights on. |
| 15 | And the ceiling above it was not flat. Not smooth. Not anything the models had drawn. It was carved into shapes. | Upward view: the underside of the ice, lit by the robot. |
| 16 | Terraces, like a staircase. Deep vertical cracks. Sloped walls. An entire hidden landscape, hanging upside down above the water. | Slow reveal of terraced, crevassed ice ceiling. |
| 17 | Then the team compared melt rates across that landscape, and the result did not match the predictions. Not even close. | Split screen: flat ice vs. crevassed ice, melt arrows. |
| 18 | Across the flat stretches, the ice was melting more slowly than expected. Slower. That was better news than anyone had hoped for. | Flat-ice panel, slow melt arrows, downward-trending figure. |
| 19 | But inside the cracks, and along the sloped walls, the melting was fast. In places, several times faster than the flat ice. | Crevasse panel, rapid melt arrows cutting into walls. |
| 20 | So the glacier was not dissolving evenly, like an ice cube in a glass. It was being unpicked, along its weak points. | Ice cube melting evenly, then a block splitting along seams. |
| 21 | That distinction matters far more than the raw numbers. Here is exactly why it changes every forecast we had. | Clean text beat over dark ice. |
| 22 | Ice that melts evenly shrinks. Slowly. Predictably. You can model that, and project a century ahead with some confidence. | Smooth shrinking-volume animation with a steady curve. |
| 23 | Ice that fails along its cracks does not shrink. It fractures. And fracturing holds, and holds, and then goes all at once. | Stress lines propagating, sudden calving. |
| 24 | Now add the ground. The bedrock slopes inland and downward, so every retreat carries the glacier into deeper water. | Grounding line retreating down the reverse slope. |
| 25 | Deeper water means more warm ocean touching more ice, which pushes the grip point back further, into deeper water again. | Same diagram, retreat repeating one step further. |
| 26 | That is a feedback loop. A system that, once it truly starts, helps itself along without needing any more push. | Circular loop arrow: retreat → warming → retreat. |
| 27 | And I want to be straight with you, because this part gets exaggerated constantly. We do not know Thwaites has crossed that line. | Plain on-screen text: "What we know / What we don't." |
| 28 | Scientists genuinely disagree on how close it is. Some models say centuries. Anyone giving you a firm date is selling something. | Divergent projection curves fanning out from today. |
| 29 | Thwaites alone holds enough ice to raise global sea level by half a metre. It also acts as a cork, holding back far more. | Thwaites highlighted, then the wider ice sheet behind it lights up. |
| 30 | We built a robot to discover our own planet wasn't shaped the way we thought. What would you send a camera into? Subscribe. | Return to the borehole, Icefin's lights receding into the dark. |

## Notes for production
- Blocks 15–20 are the payload. If any block gets a retry budget, spend it there.
- Block 27 is the honesty beat and the reason this outperforms the reference channel's usual treatment of the same material. Do not cut it for time.
- Blocks 21 and 27 are text-forward — cheap to render, and they pace the middle.
- No human faces anywhere in the shot list, by design: cheaper, faster, and it keeps the channel faceless in the visual sense too, not just the voice.
